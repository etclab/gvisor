THE THREE PLANES

  plane      recurs                   cost                 what sits on this path
  ---------  -----------------------  -------------------  ---------------------------
  Enroll     once per HOST, ever      285 ms for two       a human, typing a 3-word code
                                      ceremonies
  Connect    once per HOST PAIR       ~4 ms                nothing; cached and lazily
             (amortized, re-dialled)                       re-dialled
  Converse   once per EXCHANGE        ~0.3 ms              nothing

  The gap that matters is the last column, not the numbers: there is no row below the
  first where a human appears. That is the spec's design forcer -- "no human ceremony
  may sit on the per-exchange path" -- and CONTROL runs it rather than asserting it, by
  firing 32 exchanges concurrently with nobody watching.

  The numbers make the same point quantitatively. Machine time alone puts the ceremony
  ~3 orders of magnitude above a stream-open; counted honestly, with the seconds a human
  spends transcribing a code, it is ~5. Amortize that over a host's lifetime and the
  per-exchange cost of the trust root is zero.

  This is also why 0-RTT could be left off. What 0-RTT buys back is a round trip on the
  Connect plane, and the Connect plane already happens once per host pair. Trading
  replay resistance -- the exact threat this substrate exists to stop -- for a saving on
  a path nothing agent-paced touches would be a bad deal at any price.

READING THESE NUMBERS

  Loopback on an unloaded box. Every figure is a FLOOR: a real deployment adds a
  network to Connect and to Converse, and adds nothing to Enroll. Treat the ratios as
  the result and the absolute numbers as the best case. Across runs the medians moved
  by roughly a factor of two in both directions; nothing here is a benchmark.

  "stream-open" is one stream plus one round trip on an already-established connection,
  with no envelope built and nothing delivered. It is the number spec section 2's claim
  is about ("stream-open on a warm connection costs no round trip").

  "exchange, end to end" additionally builds and signs the envelope, runs all ten
  receive-side checks, and hands the message to the receiving postbox over a unix
  socket -- which then consults its capability authority. Most of the difference between
  the two rows is the postbox and chaind, not the transport. They are reported
  separately so neither can be blamed for the other's cost.

  The burst is 32 exchanges fired concurrently on ONE warm connection. It completes in
  well under 32 times a serial exchange, which is what QUIC's lack of cross-stream
  head-of-line blocking is for: a slow exchange delays itself and nothing else.

  What is NOT measured here: the cost of a sandbox reading a page, calling a broker, or
  being stamped by its own kernel. None of that changed since rung 0, and including it
  would obscure the delta rung 5a actually introduces. There is also no sandbox behind
  the bench: it drives the same QUIC connection, the same signed envelope and the same
  receiving proxy, with no agent at either end.
